## 1. Big Idea

3. **Story Lead / Narrative Scene** — the coffee-for-asthma anecdote, told in first person with the narrator's own skepticism voiced and then resolved. *Function:* delivers the letter's central proof-by-witnessed-demonstration in miniature. *Key Move:* the narrator's disbelief ("I looked at her like she was crazy") mirrors and pre-empts the reader's expected disbelief.

## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle 1

**Observation:** The letter delays the brand/publisher reveal until after several pages of specific proof have already been presented, rather than opening with "From Rodale Press, publisher of Prevention magazine..."
**Likely Reason:** In a skepticism-prone category (health claims), unearned trust asked for up front can trigger immediate resistance; letting content-based proof do the initial persuasive work means the eventual brand reveal lands as confirmation rather than as the reader's only reason to believe.
**General Rule:** In categories where audience skepticism toward institutional claims is high, sequence proof-by-demonstration before institutional credibility, so credibility reinforces rather than substitutes for evidence.
**Use When:** The product category has a history of overclaiming (health, wealth, self-help) and the brand, while real and legitimate, is not yet the primary reason the specific reader would trust the claim.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The brand itself is the primary reason for the reader's trust or interest (e.g., a mailing to existing loyal customers of a well-known brand) -- in that case delaying the brand reveal wastes the letter's strongest asset.
**Example From This Promotion:** "'Where did all this come from?' I asked Shirley. 'Rodale Press,' she replied. 'You know them. They publish Prevention and Organic Gardening magazines.'"

### Principle 2

**Observation:** Rather than deeply substantiating a handful of major claims, the letter stacks dozens of brief, uniformly-formatted, page-referenced micro-claims across nearly four pages.
**Likely Reason:** Volume and consistency of specific, checkable-sounding claims can create an impression of comprehensive, exhaustive proof that a smaller number of deeply-elaborated claims would not achieve, especially for a "reference book" product where breadth itself is the value proposition.
**General Rule:** For products whose core value is breadth/comprehensiveness (reference works, compilations, libraries), proof strategy can favor a high volume of brief, specifically-formatted micro-claims over depth on fewer claims, because the proof structure should mirror the product's own structure.
**Use When:** The product's core appeal genuinely is breadth or quantity of content, and the audience is more persuaded by "there's clearly a lot here" than by deep substantiation of any single item.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The product's value depends on the strength of one or two flagship claims (a single breakthrough mechanism, a single major result) -- in that case, diluting attention across dozens of minor claims would weaken rather than strengthen the pitch.
**Example From This Promotion:** The nearly four continuous pages of "Which vitamin slows down aging? ... PAGE 100" style claim/page-number units.
