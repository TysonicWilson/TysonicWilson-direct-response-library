**Big Idea:** Parents are skeptical of "we'll help you find scholarship money" pitches because of an accompanying deluge of horror stories about unfairness, competition, and wasted time, so the letter first establishes an accepted, non-controversial premise (millions of dollars in scholarship money go unclaimed because nobody asks for it) before introducing its own guarantee-backed promise, converting a generic, distrusted category claim into a specific, written, unconditional guarantee.

## 1. Big Idea

The anthology states the strategic problem directly: "As a student approaches his or her high school senior year, information about scholarships seems to pour out of the heavens onto the student and his/her parents. Accompanying that deluge is an outpouring of horror stories about the time involved in applying for scholarships, the unfairness on the part of those who award them, and the competition from hundreds of thousands of students seeking the same awards. Convincing a student or parent of the worth of a scholarship search and placement program means overcoming either ignorance or skepticism, either of which is a formidable marketing opponent." The letter's answer, per the anthology's own diagnosis, is sequencing: "Had the letter immediately launched into a promise, the reader's skepticism would not diminish. Instead, this letter begins with a premise the reader accepts in light of the claim: '... millions of dollars in scholarship money will go unclaimed.'" Only after establishing this accepted, non-self-serving premise does the letter introduce its own specific, guarantee-backed promise: "Suppose someone said to you, 'If you don't qualify for at least $100 in financial aid, I'll give you $100.' Somebody just did." The anthology credits this device directly: "Notice the dynamite exploding from the first two sentences under the subhead 'Read This ABSOLUTE Guarantee'... This guarantee, a classic of straightforward promise without any exceptions or conditions, is a delightful variation from many of the 'guarantees' we've seen."

## 8. Mechanism Analysis

The letter's stated "how it works" centers on a proprietary database-matching mechanism, explicitly contrasted against two categories of alternative: generic "scholarship sources" and school guidance counselors. The core claim is one of database scale: "Whereas most 'scholarship sources' have access to a data base of 4 to 8 billion dollars in financial aid; TFAC's data base has more than 28 billion dollars in available financial aid. Our data base is 3 to 7 times larger than the average 'scholarship sources' data base. That's why TFAC can 'guarantee financial aid' and others can only provide 'lists'." This scale claim is used to justify a structural distinction the letter draws explicitly: "As you and I both realize there is a big difference between 'a list of five to ten financial aid sources' and 'guaranteed financial aid'... A good high school guidance counselor can provide you with 'a list of five to ten financial aid sources': however, he cannot provide 'guaranteed financial aid'." The mechanism is operationalized via a two-stage information-collection process: a "Preliminary Application Form" ("just to give us basic information") followed by a longer "Guaranteed Financial Assistance Questionnaire" ("Plan to spend 20 to 30 minutes entering the information"), after which the letter states the family will receive "specific financial aid availabilities you specifically qualify for and can get" within "two to three weeks." The College Selection service operates on an analogous matching logic (a 14-question questionnaire matched against a school database), and the SAT Test Busters service is framed as a diagnostic-plus-preparation mechanism ("an actual SAT test, analysis telling you what you should review... test-taking tips for raising your overall score").

**Primary Benefit:** A specific, individually-matched list of financial-aid sources the student qualifies for, guaranteed to be worth at least $100, plus (optionally) a personalized college-match report and an SAT diagnostic/prep package.
**Deliverables:** For Service 1 — a Preliminary Application Form followed by a "Guaranteed Financial Assistance Questionnaire," returning "specific financial aid availabilities you specifically qualify for and can get" within two to three weeks. For Service 2 — a "College Selection Questionnaire" (14 questions) returning a "spiral-bound, full-scale 'College Selection and Advanced Placement Report'" listing matching colleges plus tuition, room/board, admission requirements, average SAT scores, sports, and majors. For Service 3 — "an actual SAT test, analysis telling you what you should review," plus "test-taking tips for raising your overall score."
**Bonuses:** A $100 U.S. Savings Bond is issued as part of the guarantee if the student doesn't qualify for at least $100 in aid (functioning as a guarantee sweetener rather than a signup bonus); the bundled three-service discount functions as a late-letter bonus offer via the P.S.
**Price:** Service 1: $89 within 15 days (regular $109). Service 2: $49 within 15 days (regular $59). Service 3: $89 within 15 days (regular $109). Bundle (all three): $195 within 15 days via an enclosed "Special Private Discount Certificate."
**Price Anchor:** Each service is anchored against its own stated regular/full price (Service 1: $109 vs. $89; Service 2: $100+ implied/$59 regular vs. $49; Service 3: $109 vs. $89), and the bundle is anchored against both the sum of the individual discounted prices ("$32 less than the special low prices individually") and the sum of full regular prices ("$82 less than the regular cost").

No explicit guarantee is stated within the supplied source for Service 2 or Service 3 individually. **Urgency / Scarcity:** A uniform 15-day response window applies to all three individual service discounts and to the bundled offer ("for the next 15 days only"), restated multiple times across the letter rather than stated once.
**Why the offer is positioned as superior:** The letter differentiates Service 1 from both free/low-cost "scholarship source" lists and school guidance counselors by attaching a specific, quantified, unconditional guarantee to a stated minimum dollar outcome, which the letter argues neither alternative can offer ("he cannot provide 'guaranteed financial aid'").
**Why the reader is supposed to perceive it as a no-brainer:** Because the guarantee removes financial downside risk entirely (refund plus bonus if unsuccessful) while the stated average outcome ($1200) substantially exceeds the $89 fee, and because the bundle discount rewards committing to all three related services at once rather than purchasing them separately over time.

## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle 1

**Observation:** Rather than opening with the company's own promise, the letter opens with a neutral, sympathetic, non-self-serving premise the reader already accepts (unclaimed scholarship money exists because nobody asks) before introducing the company's own guarantee.
**Likely Reason:** In a category where the target audience has documented, pre-existing skepticism (per the anthology's stated challenge of "ignorance or skepticism, either of which is a formidable marketing opponent"), opening with a claim the reader has no reason to doubt builds momentum and trust before the letter asks the reader to accept a self-interested claim from the company itself.
**General Rule:** When entering a category the target audience already associates with skepticism, wasted effort, or unfairness, consider opening with an accepted, third-party-verifiable, non-self-serving premise related to the problem before introducing the company's own promise or guarantee, so the reader's first agreement in the letter is with a neutral fact rather than a sales claim.
**Use When:** A genuinely accepted, defensible premise exists that supports the eventual promise without itself being a marketing claim (e.g., a documented category-wide phenomenon).
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** No such neutral premise exists, or the "accepted premise" is itself contestable or exaggerated, in which case opening with it risks the reader's first reaction being disagreement rather than agreement.
**Example From This Promotion:** "This year, millions of dollars in scholarship money will go unclaimed. Why? Because nobody asks for the funds in these 'hidden' scholarships."

### Principle 2

**Observation:** The letter attaches an unconditional, quantified, written guarantee to a specific minimum outcome ("at least $100 in financial aid"), rather than a vague satisfaction guarantee, and explicitly notes the guarantee is enforceable ("the post office will jump on us if we don't live up to it").
**Likely Reason:** A specific, quantified minimum outcome is easier for a skeptical reader to evaluate and trust than a vague "satisfaction guaranteed" claim, because it commits the company to a testable, falsifiable promise rather than a subjective one.
**General Rule:** When a service's outcome can be meaningfully quantified with a defensible floor (a minimum dollar amount, a minimum count, a minimum measurable result), consider guaranteeing that specific floor unconditionally rather than offering a vague satisfaction guarantee, and explicitly note any external enforcement mechanism (e.g., regulatory or postal accountability) that reinforces the guarantee's credibility.
**Use When:** The company can genuinely and reliably deliver the quantified floor for the overwhelming majority of respondents, making the guarantee's actual payout risk low and sustainable.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The quantified floor cannot be reliably delivered to most respondents, in which case the guarantee becomes either financially unsustainable or effectively hollow if enforcement is weak.
**Example From This Promotion:** "Suppose somebody said to you, 'If you don't qualify for at least $100 in financial aid, I'll give you $100.' Somebody just did... Please don't miss a word of this. It's in writing, which means the post office will jump on us if we don't live up to it."

## 17. Worth Swiping

- Opening with an accepted, non-self-serving premise related to the problem before introducing the company's own promise, in a category the audience already approaches skeptically.
- Attaching an unconditional, quantified guarantee to a specific minimum outcome rather than a vague satisfaction promise, and explicitly citing an external enforcement mechanism to reinforce credibility.
- Explicitly contrasting the offer's category against a lesser competitive category the reader might otherwise conflate it with ("Don't put TFAC in that category").
- Explicitly managing expectations around a guaranteed minimum ("$100 is just the floor") to prevent the guarantee from being misread as the full expected outcome.
- Introducing a bundled multi-service discount only after each individual service has been separately established and priced, rather than leading with the bundle.
