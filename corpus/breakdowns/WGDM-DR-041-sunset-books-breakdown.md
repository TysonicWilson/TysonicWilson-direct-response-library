## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle 1

**Observation:** The letter converts an abstract, low-differentiation product claim ("this book has good gardening information") into a long sequence of hyper-specific, individually named, page-cited micro-benefits.
**Likely Reason:** Specificity itself functions as a proxy for credibility and value when no external proof (testimonials, data, awards) is available — a large number of concrete, verifiable-sounding details creates a cumulative impression of substance that a general claim cannot.
**General Rule:** When selling an information product with no external validation to lean on, replace broad category claims with a dense sequence of specific, individually nameable contents.
**Use When:** The product's actual differentiator is breadth/organization of existing content rather than a novel mechanism, and the audience is already receptive to the category.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The audience is unaware of or skeptical toward the product category itself — a long list of specifics without first establishing why the category matters risks feeling like noise rather than persuasion.
**Example From This Promotion:** The extended sequence of named, underlined, page-cited teasers ("14 best... climbing roses... Page 24," "how to beat the budworms in July," "Star Everlasting... perfect dried flower").

### Principle 2

**Observation:** The letter stacks three separate incentives — a free trial, a guaranteed keep-no-matter-what gift, and a chance at cash — rather than relying on a single offer element.
**Likely Reason:** Layering distinct incentive types (risk removal, free value, and lottery-style urgency) appeals to different reader motivations simultaneously and creates a "nothing to lose" framing that a single incentive alone cannot achieve.
**General Rule:** When a core offer alone may not overcome inertia in a low-urgency, low-differentiation category, add a guaranteed-value element (removes downside) plus a time-limited, low-cost-to-grant incentive (creates urgency) alongside the primary trial offer.
**Use When:** The core product is genuinely low-risk to try (a free preview, cancel-anytime trial) and the marketer can afford to guarantee a small gift regardless of outcome.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The stacked incentives are so numerous they dilute the core product's own appeal or make the offer feel gimmicky rather than confident — the accompanying editorial critique itself flags a mild version of this risk, noting the P.P.S. "does remove some of the impact from the P.S."
