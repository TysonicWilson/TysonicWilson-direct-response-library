## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle

**Observation:** The letter opens by stating its two biggest objections (a $4,500 price and 14-hour days) before any benefit is introduced, then immediately labels the pivot to benefits with a short, memorable phrase ("So much for the agony: Now for the ecstasy!").
**Likely Reason:** Naming the reader's most obvious objections first, in the writer's own voice, prevents the reader from feeling like objections are being hidden or minimized, and the labeled pivot creates a clear structural signal that the justification to follow is meant to outweigh those stated costs.
**General Rule:** When selling a high-cost, high-commitment offer, consider stating the offer's most significant costs or demands upfront, in plain terms, before pivoting explicitly and memorably into the justification for why those costs are worth it.
**Use When:** The offer's benefits genuinely and substantially outweigh its stated costs, and the writer has strong, specific proof to back that claim in the sections that follow.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The offer's costs are not clearly outweighed by real, substantiated benefits — leading with stark cost/commitment language without a strong payoff risks reinforcing resistance rather than disarming it.
**Example From This Promotion:** "If you accept this proposal, it will cost your firm $4,500... you or your designate will commit to a series of 14-hour days on a college campus. So much for the agony: Now for the ecstasy!"

### Principle

**Observation:** The letter borrows credibility for a brand-new, unproven credential (PDM) by directly and repeatedly comparing it to two well-established, widely respected professional certifications (CPA, CLU) in adjacent fields.
**Likely Reason:** A new credential has no independent track record or market recognition to draw on; anchoring it explicitly against certifications the reader already respects transfers some of that pre-existing trust onto the new offering without requiring the new credential to build its own reputation from zero.
**General Rule:** When introducing a genuinely new credential, standard, or category, consider explicitly naming one or two well-established parallels the target audience already trusts, and drawing the comparison directly rather than leaving the reader to infer it.
**Use When:** The comparison is reasonably apt and the audience is already familiar with, and respects, the reference credentials being invoked.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The comparison is a stretch that a skeptical reader could easily reject, which could undermine credibility rather than build it — this device works best when the parallel is close and defensible.
**Example From This Promotion:** "it's comparable to an accountant studying for CPA certification. Or, an insurance executive going back to college to become a chartered life underwriter (CLU). CPS, CLU, PDM."

## 17. Worth Swiping

- Leading with the offer's biggest objections (price, time commitment) before pivoting explicitly into justification, rather than downplaying or delaying them.
- Anchoring a new, unproven credential or offering against one or two well-established parallels the audience already respects.
- Stacking multiple distinct proof types (institutional, faculty-credential, named-alumni social proof) rather than relying on a single proof source, especially for higher-price, higher-skepticism offers.
- Quantifying scarcity precisely (a specific total capacity, a specific personally-controlled subset) rather than using vague "limited enrollment" language.
- Revealing the seller's own personal stake or emotional investment ("Enthralled comes closer") immediately before the scarcity mechanism, to make the limited-availability offer feel like a personal opportunity rather than a generic sales tactic.
- Offering an unusually high-trust, personal contact channel (an unlisted phone number) as a final urgency and access device.
