## 1. Big Idea

The letter's persuasive core rests on stacking and summing: rather than presenting a single mileage benefit, it itemizes three separately-triggered bonuses (acceptance, spend-match, flight-completion) and then performs the addition for the reader — "That adds up to 11,000 bonus miles." This does two things simultaneously: it makes the offer feel richer than a single number would (three distinct "wins" rather than one), and it removes any arithmetic burden from the reader. The letter then immediately converts that abstract total into a concrete, desirable unit — "more than half of what you need for your first free domestic travel award" — so the reader doesn't have to independently understand mileage-redemption economics to grasp the offer's value. The underlying product is a standard co-branded travel rewards card; the marketing interpretation is a compounding, self-summing bonus structure paired with instant reward translation.

## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle 1

**Observation:** The letter itemizes multiple separately-conditioned bonuses and then explicitly performs the summation in copy, rather than leaving the reader to add the components themselves.
**Likely Reason:** Doing the arithmetic for the reader removes a small but real point of friction and ensures the headline number (used at both the open and the sum) lands with the intended impact.
**General Rule:** When an offer is genuinely composed of multiple stacked incentives, state each component individually for credibility, then explicitly sum them into one headline figure so the reader is not required to do mental math to grasp the offer's total value.
**Use When:** The offer legitimately has multiple, independently-triggered bonus components that are individually modest but collectively substantial.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The individual components are trivial or the summed total would mislead about typical/expected value (e.g., if most respondents could not realistically qualify for all stacked components) — the summation should reflect a genuinely achievable total, not a best-case-only figure.
**Example From This Promotion:** "You'll get 4,000 bonus miles just for accepting the card... Charge your first $1,000... an additional 1,000 bonus miles. Fly round-trip transatlantic... 6,000 additional bonus miles. That adds up to 11,000 bonus miles."

### Principle 2

**Observation:** An abstract quantitative benefit (mileage total) is immediately translated into proximity toward a concrete, desirable outcome (a free ticket) rather than left as a standalone number.
**Likely Reason:** Most readers do not have an intuitive sense of what a given mileage total is "worth"; translating it into "more than half of what you need" gives the number instant, concrete meaning without requiring the reader to already understand the loyalty program's redemption economics.
**General Rule:** When an offer's core benefit is expressed in a program-specific unit unfamiliar to a general audience, immediately benchmark that unit against a well-known, desirable milestone to make its value legible.
**Use When:** The audience is program-aware but the specific total offered is new or unusual, and a clear redemption threshold exists to anchor against.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The redemption threshold itself is unstable, frequently changed, or would require a disclaimer that undercuts the claim's cleanliness — anchoring against a moving target can create a compliance or credibility risk.
**Example From This Promotion:** "That adds up to 11,000 bonus miles - more than half of what you need for your first free domestic travel award."
