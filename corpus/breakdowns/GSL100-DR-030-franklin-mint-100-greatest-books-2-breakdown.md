## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle

**Observation:** The letter is deliberately brief and functions specifically as a personal, executive-signed cover note that explicitly defers detailed persuasion to a separate, accompanying letter, rather than attempting to make the full sales case itself.
**Likely Reason:** Separating a short, high-trust, personal referral note from the detailed, longer sales argument allows each piece to do a distinct job well -- the short note transfers existing brand trust and creates urgency efficiently, while the longer letter can carry the full evidentiary and emotional case without diluting the personal note's impact.
**General Rule:** When introducing an existing, trusted customer base to a genuinely new product line or division, consider pairing a brief, personal, executive-signed cover note (leveraging existing trust) with a separate, fuller sales letter that carries the detailed persuasive argument, rather than combining both functions into a single letter.
**Use When:** There is a genuine, credible executive or authority figure available to sign the cover note, and the existing customer relationship is strong enough to make the brief endorsement meaningful.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** No genuine existing trust relationship exists with the recipient, in which case a brief, detail-light cover note risks reading as insufficiently substantive to a cold audience.
**Example From This Promotion:** "This letter is signed by the publisher of The Franklin Library, a new division of The Franklin Mint which we have established to revive the great tradition of publishing limited-edition collections of fine books in superb bindings... In the accompanying letter, you will learn about another form of collecting..."

### Principle

**Observation:** The letter uses honestly hedged superlative language ("I believe it may well be the most beautiful collection of books to be published in our lifetime") rather than an unqualified absolute claim.
**Likely Reason:** Hedged superlatives attributed to a named, accountable individual read as more considered and genuine than unqualified marketing absolutes, while still delivering strong persuasive weight through the superlative framing itself.
**General Rule:** When making a bold, superlative claim on behalf of an executive or authority figure, consider softening it with honest hedging language ("I believe," "may well be") rather than stating it as an unqualified fact, to preserve credibility while retaining persuasive impact.
**Use When:** The claim is genuinely a matter of opinion or judgment (e.g., aesthetic or subjective quality assessments) rather than a verifiable, objective fact.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The underlying claim is objectively verifiable and the hedging would unnecessarily weaken a claim that could be stated with full confidence.
**Example From This Promotion:** "I bring it to your attention because I believe it may well be the most beautiful collection of books to be published in our lifetime."
