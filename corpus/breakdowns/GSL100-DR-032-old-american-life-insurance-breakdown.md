## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle

**Observation:** The letter opens with a direct, self-consciously blunt question about the reader's own past inaction ("Are you satisfied with what you have done about it?"), explicitly acknowledging its own bluntness ("I'm sorry if that question seems blunt") before immediately offering a concrete, low-friction solution.
**Likely Reason:** For a warm prospect who has already expressed interest but not acted, directly naming the unresolved gap between stated interest and actual behavior can be a stronger motivator than a softer re-approach, and acknowledging the question's bluntness pre-empts a defensive reaction.
**General Rule:** When re-approaching a warm prospect who has previously expressed interest but not converted, consider directly and personally naming the gap between their stated interest and their lack of action, paired with an immediate, low-friction next step and an acknowledgment that the direct approach may feel blunt.
**Use When:** The prospect genuinely has a documented prior expression of interest, making the confrontation feel personally relevant rather than presumptuous.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The prospect has no established prior relationship or interest signal, in which case the same confrontational framing would likely feel presumptuous or off-putting to a cold audience.

### Principle

**Observation:** The letter transparently discloses specific underwriting limitations (the two-year contestability period, pre-existing condition exclusions, suicide exclusions) using explicit policy-provision-style language, rather than omitting or vaguely referencing these terms.
**Likely Reason:** Presenting limitations in specific, contract-like language (rather than omitting them or describing them vaguely) builds trust by demonstrating the offer is not hiding unfavorable terms, which can be especially important for a product category (insurance) where hidden exclusions are a common source of consumer distrust.
**General Rule:** When selling a product with genuine contractual limitations or exclusions, consider disclosing them in specific, precise language rather than vague or omitted references, even when doing so introduces necessary complexity into the letter.
**Use When:** The limitations are a genuine, standard part of the product and disclosing them transparently is both accurate and required for informed consent.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** Excessive technical/legal detail would overwhelm or confuse the specific audience being addressed, in which case a simplified but still honest summary may serve better than full contract language.
