## Executive Breakdown

**Big Idea:** Homeowners already accept that insurance premiums are simply a non-refundable, ongoing cost of protection, so rather than compete on price or coverage breadth, the letter introduces an entirely new product category — a single up-front deposit that is fully refunded, regardless of claims history, if the policy is ever canceled by either party — opened with a disarming "What if..." question that lets the reader imagine the concept before the company or its credibility is revealed.
**Big Promise:** Make a single up-front deposit (lump sum or financed) instead of paying annual homeowners insurance premiums, and get back every cent of that deposit if the policy is ever canceled, no matter how many or how large the claims filed in the meantime.
**Underlying Reality:** An established, 200-plus-year-old mutual insurance company is marketing a homeowners insurance product structured around a large up-front deposit rather than recurring premiums, with the deposit functioning economically similarly to a refundable security deposit rather than a traditional non-refundable premium, and financing options available for homeowners who cannot pay the full deposit at once.
**Primary Lead Type:** Secret — the letter opens with an unattributed, hypothetical "What if..." scenario before revealing what company or product is actually being discussed: "What if... ...what if you could make a single payment to cover your homeowners insurance... ...then get back every dime should you or the company ever cancel your policy? **Core Mechanism:** A single up-front deposit (paid as a lump sum or financed through a stated "special home equity financing program," with interest described as tax-deductible) replaces annual premium payments; the full deposit is refunded ("every cent") if the policy is ever canceled by the policyholder or by the company, described explicitly as holding true "no matter how many claims you may have filed."
**Primary Proof Strategy:** A stated third-party credibility rating (A.M. Best A+ "Superior") combined with a company-age claim (founded 1784, "more than two centuries") and a single embedded customer quote, rather than statistical performance data.

## 1. Big Idea

The anthology states the strategic problem directly: "Selling insurance by mail is challenge enough. Selling a totally new concept in homeowners insurance magnifies the challenge geometrically. This is not an easy concept. Shortly into the letter, the writer says, 'May I explain?' What follows will either make the challenge unbeatable or wash it away." The letter's chosen solution is to present the concept as a scenario the reader is invited to imagine before learning who is behind it, using an explicitly reader-involving question structure. The anthology's commentary on this technique is direct: "'What if ...' is an ideal way to begin a letter, because it combines two reader-involvement devices: the question, which, unless grotesquely misused, has to involve the reader; and the non-threatening teaser. The non-threatening teaser differs from teasers which throw the reader into a new problem, by allowing the reader to believe he or she is simply observing a circumstance... while actually being drawn into a circumstance. So 'what if' used as professionally as this writer has used it is a happy solution for a writer selling a service the reader might quickly reject if that service were presented through straight exposition." The letter's actual big idea is then stated directly: "TWO big benefits -- one of them a 'minor miracle'! The 'miracle' is this: Should you cancel your policy (or should The Green Tree cancel it, for that matter) you get back every cent. Every cent. That holds true no matter how many claims you may have filed. Has any insurance company ever extended an offer like that to you?"

## 8. Mechanism Analysis

The letter's stated "how it works" centers on inverting the conventional insurance-premium structure. Instead of recurring, non-refundable annual premium payments, the policyholder makes a single, larger up-front deposit: "My offer to you makes it possible for you to make a single up-front deposit. You don't pay annual premiums." The core differentiating mechanism is the refund condition, restated twice for emphasis: "Should you cancel your policy (or should The Green Tree cancel it, for that matter) you get back every cent... That holds true no matter how many claims you may have filed," and again on page two: "Whether you pay in one lump sum or finance your deposit over a period of time, your deposit is always guaranteed refundable. That's true regardless of the number of your claims or how big your claims might be." A secondary mechanism addresses the practical affordability problem the larger deposit creates: a "special home equity financing program" allows the deposit to be paid over time rather than as a single lump sum, with the letter noting the financing's interest payments are "tax-deductible" and that the entire financed deposit amount remains refundable on the same claims-independent basis as a lump-sum payment. The letter explicitly acknowledges the deposit's necessarily larger size relative to a single annual premium ("your up-front deposit has to be larger than a single annual premium would be") rather than obscuring this trade-off.

## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle 1

**Observation:** Rather than naming the unfamiliar product or company upfront, the letter opens with an unattributed, hypothetical "What if..." question, letting the reader engage with the concept before any sales framing or company identity is introduced.
**Likely Reason:** A genuinely novel, hard-to-believe concept is more likely to be dismissed if presented as a direct claim from an unknown company; framing it first as a hypothetical the reader is simply "observing" lowers defensiveness and allows the idea itself to be evaluated on its own merits before the reader has to decide whether to trust the messenger.
**General Rule:** When introducing a genuinely novel product structure or claim that would likely trigger immediate skepticism if stated directly and attributed to the company from the first sentence, consider opening with a non-threatening, hypothetical framing device (e.g., "What if...") that lets the reader engage with the idea before the company's identity or sales intent is revealed.
**Use When:** The underlying claim, once explained, is genuinely true and defensible — the "What if" device only defers skepticism, it does not resolve it, so the subsequent explanation must actually deliver on the premise.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The claim is not actually as advertised once explained, or the audience's skepticism is warranted, in which case the reader's eventual disappointment upon the reveal likely damages trust more than a direct claim would have.
**Example From This Promotion:** "What if... ...what if you could make a single payment to cover your homeowners insurance... ...then get back every dime should you or the company ever cancel your policy? You say such coverage just doesn't exist? For most homeowners, you'd be right."

### Principle 2

**Observation:** The letter pairs a specific, checkable historical fact (a 1784 founding date) with a specific, named, independent industry rating (A.M. Best A+ Superior) rather than relying on self-referential superlatives alone.
**Likely Reason:** Self-referential claims ("we're the best," "most respected") are easily dismissed as marketing language; pairing them with independently verifiable facts (a specific founding year, a named external rating agency and tier) gives the reader concrete, checkable anchors that lend the surrounding superlative language more weight than it would carry alone.
**General Rule:** When making a broad credibility claim (trustworthy, respected, reliable), pair it with at least one specific, independently verifiable fact — a precise founding date, a named third-party rating or certification with its exact tier — rather than relying on the superlative language alone.
**Use When:** A genuine, checkable, favorable third-party fact or rating actually exists and can be named specifically.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** No genuine independent verification exists, or the actual rating/fact is unfavorable or irrelevant, in which case citing it either isn't possible or would undermine rather than support the credibility claim.
**Example From This Promotion:** "The Green Tree name is not only one of the most respected in the world of insurance; it's also one of the oldest, going back more than 200 years (founded 1784)... America's leading insurance analyst, A.M. Best Company, ranks The Green Tree A+ (Superior) for reliability and quality of service — the highest rating Best gives."

## 17. Worth Swiping

- Opening a hard-to-believe or genuinely novel offer with a non-threatening hypothetical ("What if...") that lets the reader engage with the concept before the company's identity or sales intent is revealed.
- Explicitly voicing the reader's most likely skeptical reaction ("You say such coverage just doesn't exist?") before asking permission to explain further, rather than plowing ahead unprompted.
- Pairing self-referential credibility claims with at least one specific, independently verifiable fact (a founding date, a named third-party rating and exact tier).
- Restating a key unconditional term (e.g., "regardless of claims") more than once across the letter, at different points in the argument, rather than stating it only once and moving on.
- Closing on a category-reframing phrase (e.g., "turning an expense into an asset") that gives the reader a compact mental label summarizing the letter's core argument.
