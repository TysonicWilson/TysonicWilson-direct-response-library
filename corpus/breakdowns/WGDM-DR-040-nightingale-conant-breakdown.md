## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle 1 — Confess the Reader's Likely Flaw Through the Signer's Own Story First

**Observation:** Rather than telling the reader she makes interpersonal mistakes, the letter has the signer confess his own specific, relatable past mistakes in detail, letting the reader privately recognize similar patterns in herself.
**Likely Reason:** Directly accusing a reader of a personal shortcoming risks triggering defensiveness and rejection of the message — the book's own commentary flags this exact risk ("causing irritation instead of acceptance"); a confessional story sidesteps this by making the "confession" belong to the writer, not an accusation aimed at the reader.
**General Rule:** When a promotion's core premise requires the reader to recognize an unflattering truth about herself, consider having the signer confess a specific, relatable version of that same flaw first, rather than stating the flaw as a direct claim about the reader.
**Use When:** The target problem is one readers are likely to resist acknowledging directly (skill gaps, personal shortcomings, embarrassing habits) but would recognize readily if shown a specific, non-accusatory example.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The reader already fully recognizes and wants to solve the problem (a direct problem statement would be faster and equally effective, without needing the extra narrative setup).
**Example From This Promotion:** "If to err is human, then nobody's more human than I am. I have in my time made every mistake in the book in dealing with people," followed by the cocktail-party and dry-cleaner anecdotes.

### Principle 2 — Escalate Proof from Personal/Relatable to Historically/Financially Monumental

**Observation:** The letter's proof structure moves in stages — from small, personal, low-stakes anecdotes (a stained coat, a missed work deadline) to historically enormous, high-stakes examples (Rockefeller's fortune, Schwab's unprecedented salary) — immediately before presenting the offer.
**Likely Reason:** ANALYTICAL INFERENCE — personal anecdotes build relatability and belief that the technique works in ordinary life, while the subsequent historical/financial proof establishes that the underlying skill itself is extraordinarily valuable, giving the reader both "this works for people like me" and "this skill is worth a fortune" before being asked to spend $99.95.
**General Rule:** When proof serves two different jobs — demonstrating that a technique works, and establishing that the underlying skill is highly valuable — sequence relatable, small-scale proof first, then escalate to historically or financially dramatic proof immediately before the offer.
**Use When:** The product teaches a skill or capability whose ultimate value can be illustrated through both everyday relatable examples and larger historical or celebrity-scale examples.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The larger-scale proof examples (as here, an unnamed engineering-college statistic) cannot be sourced or verified — using unverifiable statistics for the "escalated" proof risks undermining the credibility built by the earlier, more grounded proof.
**Example From This Promotion:** The dry-cleaner and workplace anecdotes, followed immediately by "The Skill Worth More Than Any Other" section featuring the Rockefeller quote and the Schwab salary example.
