## 8. Mechanism Analysis

The letter's stated mechanism is a three-part filter for identifying "hyper-growth" stocks before Wall Street recognizes their potential: (1) the company must be using or creating a genuinely disruptive (revolutionary) technology; (2) that technology must deliver vastly lower prices to consumers, not merely incremental improvement; (3) the company's success must still generate meaningful skepticism among mainstream Wall Street analysts, indicating the market has not yet priced in its full potential. The letter argues this pattern recurs across otherwise unrelated industries and eras — 19th-century railroads, Standard Oil's kerosene pricing, Ford's Model T assembly line, Intel's semiconductor price collapse (Moore's Law), Nucor's steel-casting technology, Dell's direct-sales model, and Amazon.com's online retail pricing — and is presently visible again in fiber-optic telecommunications infrastructure ("Internet II"), which the letter argues is displacing legacy carriers like AT&T by delivering vastly cheaper long-distance and data capacity. The letter explicitly critiques conventional Wall Street valuation methods (P/E ratios, debt-to-equity ratios) as "old paradigms" unsuited to identifying this pattern, positioning its three-criteria framework as a differentiated, proprietary-feeling analytical lens rather than a specific proprietary data source or algorithm — the "mechanism" being sold is fundamentally an interpretive framework applied by the author's own judgment, illustrated by a self-reported track record rather than a disclosed, mechanical, third-party-auditable model.

## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle 1

**Observation:** The letter opens with two vivid, second-person "imagine yourself" historical scenes (1850 railroad investor, 1908 Detroit banker) before revealing the identity of the historical figures involved (Henry Ford), rather than naming the well-known example upfront.
**Likely Reason:** Placing the reader inside an unlabeled scenario first, then revealing a familiar, credible name at the end of the story, creates a small "aha" moment that reinforces the letter's core argument (this pattern is real and has famous, verifiable examples) more memorably than stating the famous name at the outset.
**General Rule:** When using a well-known historical or business example as proof, consider withholding the specific name until after building a vivid, immersive scene around the underlying situation, letting the reveal function as a credibility payoff rather than opening with the name and diminishing the narrative's suspense.
**Use When:** The eventual reveal is a genuinely well-known, credible, and verifiable name or company that will land with recognition and added weight once revealed.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The eventual reveal is obscure or not independently verifiable — withholding a name only to reveal something unfamiliar or unconfirmable can feel like a bait-and-switch rather than a satisfying payoff.
**Example From This Promotion:** "You're backing a young, wild-eyed mechanic, whose new system for making motorcars promises to revolutionize the industry... It's a true story — that 'wild-eyed mechanic' is Henry Ford."

### Principle 2

**Observation:** The letter explicitly applies its own stated analytical framework (the three-criteria hyper-growth test) to a well-known stock (AOL) and shows it failing, rather than only ever presenting positive confirming examples of the framework.
**Likely Reason:** A framework that is shown to say "no" to at least one plausible, well-known candidate demonstrates real discriminating power and appears more rigorous and honest than a framework that seems to validate every example it's applied to.
**General Rule:** When presenting a proprietary evaluative framework or methodology as proof of a system's validity, include at least one clear negative/disqualifying example alongside the positive confirming examples, to demonstrate the framework has genuine selectivity rather than being retrofitted to justify a predetermined conclusion.
**Use When:** A genuine, well-known negative example exists that plausibly could have seemed to fit the pattern but doesn't, and the reasoning for exclusion is specific and defensible.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** No credible negative example is available, or the "failure" case is too obscure or contrived to be recognizable/meaningful to the reader — a negative example only works as proof if the reader already has some familiarity with (and possibly some assumption about) the disqualified example.
**Example From This Promotion:** "Even a cursory look at America Online, will show you that it doesn't meet the criteria... First, there's nothing truly disruptive about dial-up Internet access... And finally, there's no suspicion left about AOL — it's Main Street's investment choice for an online stock."

### Principle 3

**Observation:** The letter cites a specific instance where the author's own past recommendation directly and explicitly contradicted a major, named, mainstream financial publication's prediction (a Barron's cover story forecasting Kodak would rise to $100, versus the author's short recommendation), and reports the author's position as the one that was proven correct.
**Likely Reason:** Contrasting a specific, falsifiable personal call against a credible, well-known, and independently checkable third-party prediction — rather than against a vague or unnamed competitor — creates a much sharper, higher-stakes proof point than a generic "I was right" claim, because the reader has an external, familiar reference point to judge the claim against.
**General Rule:** When citing a past track-record win as proof, look for instances where the position taken explicitly and specifically contradicted a named, credible, mainstream source's stated prediction, and cite both the contradiction and the outcome together.
**Use When:** A genuine, verifiable instance of this kind exists, and citing the specific contradicting mainstream source doesn't require exaggerating or misrepresenting what that source actually said.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** No such specific, verifiable contradiction exists — inventing or exaggerating an implied "disagreement" with a mainstream source that didn't actually make the claimed prediction would be a fabricated proof point and a serious credibility risk.
**Example From This Promotion:** "In July 1998, I recommended selling short shares of Kodak, citing falling sales and market share. I reiterated this recommendation even after a Barron's cover story was published the following August predicting that Kodak's shares would go to $100. Shares of Kodak fell from $86 to $64 for gains of 25%."
