## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle: Segment identical underlying product science into audience-specific outcome framings

**Observation:** The same core mechanism and even the same named scientific study appear in both this letter and its companion (WGDM-DR-029), but this letter reframes the outcome as beauty/appearance (radiant skin, lustrous hair) while the companion frames it as general health/vitality — matched to each list segment's stated interest (beauty-aids catalog vs. general health catalog).
**Likely Reason:** The underlying product truth doesn't change, but what a given reader cares about does; presenting the identical antioxidant/free-radical mechanism through the specific outcome lens each list segment already self-selected for (by requesting a differently-advertised version of the same catalog) maximizes relevance without requiring two different products.
**General Rule:** When a single product mechanism supports multiple distinct outcome benefits (e.g., an ingredient that affects both general health and appearance), segment your list by demonstrated interest and write outcome-specific versions of the same underlying proof, rather than writing one generic letter that tries to cover every benefit for every reader.
**Use When:** You have genuine list-segmentation data (as this company did, per the compilation's commentary, from tracking which catalog advertisement/placement a given inquiry came from) and the underlying product genuinely supports multiple distinct, honestly-claimable outcomes.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** No genuine segmentation data exists, or the "different" outcome framing would require overstating what the product's proof actually supports for that specific audience.
**Example From This Promotion:** The identical Ohio State University free-radical study is used to support a general "1,000 times more potent than vitamin C or E" claim in the companion letter and a beauty-specific "verified by modern science... helps restore balance... revive your youthful glow" claim here.

### Principle: Use a guided sensory self-assessment to generate a felt problem, rather than asserting the problem directly

**Observation:** Rather than telling the reader her hair or skin has a problem, the letter walks her through a specific physical self-check (touch your hair, touch your face, look in the mirror) and lets her draw her own conclusion.
**Likely Reason:** A reader-generated observation ("my hair does feel a little coarse") is more persuasive and less resistance-triggering than an externally-asserted claim ("your hair is damaged"), because the reader arrives at the felt problem through her own senses rather than being told what to feel.
**General Rule:** When a product addresses a subjective, self-perceivable condition (appearance, comfort, energy level), open with a guided self-assessment sequence that prompts the reader to notice the relevant condition herself, rather than asserting the problem exists.
**Use When:** The condition being addressed is genuinely self-perceivable and the reader can meaningfully assess it in the moment of reading (touch, sight, immediate physical sensation).
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The condition is not something the reader can directly self-assess (e.g., an internal medical condition requiring diagnosis), where a self-check device would be inappropriate or misleading.
**Example From This Promotion:** "Take a half-second to run your fingers through your hair... Does it feel silky and strong? Or does it feel a little coarse and brittle?"
