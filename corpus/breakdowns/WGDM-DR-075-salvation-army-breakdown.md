## 1. Big Idea

The anthology states the strategic problem with unusual directness: "Does a camp for blind children seem to be a worthy competitor for the fund-raising dollar? The concept itself is foreign to many. In an appeal-crowded circumstance, the writer's challenge is to make this entreaty one that penetrates typical recipient-apathy." Rather than argue the cause's merit through statistics or need-based appeal, the letter's solution begins before the letter is even opened, on its exterior folded panels: "BEFORE YOU READ THIS LETTER PLEASE SHUT YOUR EYES TIGHT FOR JUST 30 SECONDS." Unfolding further reveals: "WHAT IF THAT HAD BEEN 30 YEARS?" The anthology's own assessment of this device: "In this instance, the outer fold, which might have been left blank, becomes a potent introduction to the letter itself... The letter generates a haunting feeling which can be relieved only by mailing a donation. And by achieving that extraordinary effect, it becomes one of the great fund raising letters of all time." The letter's actual central image, introduced once the reader opens it, distills the idea further: a rope strung around camp becomes, for blind campers, a "Life-line" — a tangible symbol of the freedom and independence donor support provides.

## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle 1

**Observation:** The letter's most powerful persuasive device (the eyes-shut instruction) occurs on the physical exterior of the folded letter, before the reader has even opened it or encountered any body copy.
**Likely Reason:** For a cause the anthology itself calls "foreign to many," conventional attention-getting devices within the letter body (a bold headline, an opening statistic) still require the reader to have already opened and begun reading; placing an interactive, experiential device on the letter's own physical exterior captures engagement at the earliest possible moment, before the reader has decided whether the letter is worth opening at all.
**General Rule:** When a cause or product is unfamiliar enough that conventional in-letter hooks risk never being read, consider whether the physical format of the mail piece itself (its fold, its exterior panel, its envelope) can carry an interactive or experiential device that engages the reader before the letter is even opened.
**Use When:** The mailing format allows for a folded or multi-panel physical structure, and a genuinely simple, safe, brief experiential instruction exists that meaningfully relates to the cause (a sensory deprivation, a physical action, a specific short wait).
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The format doesn't support this kind of physical interaction (a single flat page, a digital-only channel), or the experiential device would be gimmicky rather than genuinely connected to the cause's actual substance.
**Example From This Promotion:** "BEFORE YOU READ THIS LETTER PLEASE SHUT YOUR EYES TIGHT FOR JUST 30 SECONDS" followed by "WHAT IF THAT HAD BEEN 30 YEARS?"

### Principle 2

**Observation:** The letter builds its entire persuasive case around a single, concrete physical artifact (the rope) rather than describing the camp program's full scope, statistics, or operations.
**Likely Reason:** A single, vivid, personified image is easier for a donor to remember, feel, and mentally attach their contribution to than a general description of "camp services" or aggregate statistics, especially for a cause the audience has no prior mental model for.
**General Rule:** When introducing an unfamiliar cause or program, identify the single most concrete, tangible element of the actual on-the-ground experience and build the letter's central image and metaphor around that specific artifact, rather than attempting to summarize the program's full scope.
**Use When:** A genuine, specific, sensorially-vivid element of the program exists that can bear symbolic weight without distortion or exaggeration.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** No single element is genuinely representative or vivid enough to carry the full weight of the appeal, or when the cause's persuasive case actually depends more on scale/statistics (e.g., disaster relief, where the scope itself is the persuasive fact) than on a single intimate image.
**Example From This Promotion:** "But Edna Sue, one of the sightless, calls the rope 'My Life-line' — because it makes life in the out-of-doors, with its smell of grass, sound of birds, and feel of nature, a taste of heaven for her."
