## 1. Big Idea

The literal subject is a diesel-powered sedan. The marketing idea reframes the purchase decision as a referendum on trusting expert opinion versus trusting real-world evidence. By opening with a direct quote of industry experts dismissing the car ("'Forget it, Heinz'... 'It just won't sell here.'"), the letter sets up a challenge it then resolves with data: real owners, driving real miles, reporting real satisfaction. The reader is invited to participate in disproving the same skepticism the experts voiced, turning the purchase decision into an act of independent judgment rather than conformity.

## 4. Lead Analysis

**Primary Lead Type:** Story lead — opens with a first-person quoted anecdote of experts advising against selling the car in America ("'Forget it, Heinz,' the experts told me").
**Secondary Lead Characteristics:** Functions simultaneously as a proclamation/contrarian lead (the letter's entire premise is proving a confident prediction wrong) and incorporates embedded testimonial proof mid-letter.
**Why This Lead Works Here:** Quoting a direct dismissal creates immediate narrative tension (will the experts be proven right or wrong?) and positions the brand as the underdog defying conventional wisdom, which is a stronger hook than a standard feature-benefit opening for a product facing a well-known objection (price + noise).
**Likely Reader State:** An American car buyer aware of the price premium and stereotype around diesel/German cars, skeptical but open to being persuaded by evidence rather than by claims alone.

## 5. Structural Map

2. **Contrarian Reveal** — "I had reason to believe the experts were wrong. Some Americans have paid $4,068 for this German car with the 'noisy engine.'" *Function:* immediately counter the hook with a factual claim.
3. **Explanation of the Noise "Objection"** — reframes the noise as something owners discover and come to accept or even enjoy: "many of these Americans wouldn't have found out about the car [if not for the noise]... convinced them the Mercedes-Benz Diesel is a great car." *Function:* neutralize the primary sensory objection by reframing it as a curiosity-driver rather than a dealbreaker.

11. **Close/CTA** — return the enclosed card for a personal test drive and a brochure ("The Amazing 190D"). *Function:* low-friction next step (test drive, not purchase).
12. **Deadline/Scarcity** — "My offer expires Monday, August 16, 1965, and is limited to the first 1,000 people who respond." *Function:* hard deadline plus hard quantity cap, a dual scarcity device.

## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle

**Observation:** The letter opens by quoting a specific, named skeptical prediction from "experts" before disproving it with evidence.
**Likely Reason:** Naming the strongest counter-argument upfront, in the opposition's own words, builds credibility (the letter isn't hiding from the objection) and creates narrative tension that the subsequent proof resolves.
**General Rule:** When a product faces a well-known, widely-shared objection, open by voicing that objection as directly and specifically as possible (ideally as a quote) before presenting evidence that contradicts it.
**Use When:** The objection is genuinely well-known to the target audience and the product has real evidence capable of countering it.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The proof to follow is weak or anecdotal — voicing a strong objection without strong evidence to counter it can reinforce the objection rather than dissolve it.
**Example From This Promotion:** "'Forget it, Heinz,' the experts told me. 'It Just won't sell here.'"

### Principle

**Observation:** The letter explains the financial mechanism behind an unusually generous incentive offer (free fuel/maintenance) immediately after presenting it, rather than leaving it unexplained.
**Likely Reason:** An offer that seems too generous can trigger suspicion; explaining the underlying economics (fuel efficiency makes the free-fuel cost low for the manufacturer) converts the offer itself into additional proof of the product's core claim.
**General Rule:** When making an unusually generous or surprising offer, explain the underlying reason it is financially sustainable, turning the offer into reinforcing proof rather than an unexplained giveaway.
**Use When:** The offer's generosity is genuinely tied to a real product advantage that can be explained credibly.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The explanation would reveal the offer as a loss-leader with no real connection to product quality, which could undermine rather than reinforce trust.
**Example From This Promotion:** "No other manufacturer... could afford to make this offer. I can make it because the Mercedes-Benz 190 Diesel averages over 30 miles per gallon..."
