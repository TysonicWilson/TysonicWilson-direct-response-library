**Observation:** Rather than arguing broadly that staying informed matters, the letter substantiates its value proposition almost entirely through an unusually long, dense sequence of specific, itemized, named examples — specific foundations (Ford, MacArthur, Weinberg, Wallace), specific cities and organizations (a San Diego shelter, a Detroit non-profit), specific regulatory topics (IRS audits, postal-rate changes), and a specific named third-party endorsement (the Grantsmanship Center).

**General Rule:** When selling an information product to an audience that already considers itself expert in the subject matter, prioritize an unusually dense sequence of specific, named, checkable examples over general claims about the value of information — let the reader's own expertise do the verification work rather than asking them to simply trust the claim.
**Use When:** The product genuinely does contain the kind of specific, dense, real content being previewed, and the target audience has enough domain expertise to recognize and value that specificity when they see it.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The audience lacks sufficient domain expertise to distinguish genuinely specific, valuable examples from generic-sounding but hollow ones, in which case density of detail alone may not persuade as effectively as a clearer, simpler benefit statement.
