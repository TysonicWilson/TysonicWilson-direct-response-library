## 1. Big Idea

This letter's distinguishing structural choice is placing a complete benefit-question overline above the salutation, then opening the letter proper not with the offer but with a specific, named story of failure: "The trip was a disaster for RVers Bob and Helen Wallace. With their perky-tailed beagle, Scruff, they'd eagerly set forth for the fun park they'd discovered last year... Two days, 800 miles, 90 gallons of gas later -- the Wallace's dreams were dashed. The park was closed." Only after fully dramatizing this concrete failure does the letter generalize it into a stated pattern ("Bob and Helen made that classic mistake too many RVers do. They relied on an old out-of-date directory") and pivot to the offer. This story-before-sale structure works specifically because the audience is existing members: the anthology notes that "To 'cold lists,' the letter probably would have been stronger without the preface, opening simply with 'The trip was a disaster for RVers Bob and Helen Wallace.' But this letter is to existing members, and projecting a new benefit is a logical beginning." The underlying reality — an annual reference book — is marketed not on its content alone but on the emotional and financial cost of not having the current edition, reframed against a "tightening energy squeeze" that makes gas waste feel newly consequential.

## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle 1

**Observation:** The letter opens with a pre-salutation overline stacking direct benefit questions, before the letter proper begins with a separate story-based lead addressed to "Dear Good Samer."
**Likely Reason:** Per the anthology, the overline "builds acceptance" for the story and offer that follow, pre-framing the reader with the letter's key benefits before the narrative section asks for a larger reading investment.
**General Rule:** When addressing an audience already favorably disposed toward the sender (existing members/customers), a benefit-question overline placed above the salutation can pre-condition the reader to receive the subsequent story or argument more receptively, functioning as a preview that reduces the perceived risk of continuing to read.
**Use When:** The mailing targets an existing, warm audience and the letter's body uses a slower-build lead type (story, problem-solution) that benefits from an upfront benefit preview.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The audience is cold and unfamiliar with the sender — per the anthology's own point, "To 'cold lists,' the letter probably would have been stronger without the preface" — an overline built on assumed rapport can feel presumptuous or confusing to a first-time reader.
**Example From This Promotion:** "Want to know how to beat the tightening energy squeeze? Learn where and how to get GUARANTEED DISCOUNTS at thousands of campgrounds & service centers across the country?"

### Principle 2

**Observation:** The letter dramatizes an abstract product-failure risk (an outdated directory) through a specific, named-character story with precise quantified consequences (two days, 800 miles, 90 gallons of gas) before generalizing it into a stated "classic mistake."
**Likely Reason:** A concrete, sensorially detailed story of a relatable third party's failure lets the reader experience the cost of inaction vicariously, without the letter needing to directly accuse the reader of the same mistake.
**General Rule:** When selling a product whose core value is preventing an abstract or hard-to-visualize failure (e.g., outdated information, missed savings, avoidable errors), dramatize that failure through a specific character and quantified consequence before generalizing it into a named, avoidable pattern the reader can now recognize themselves as being at risk of.
**Use When:** The product's core benefit is prevention of a diffuse, hard-to-quantify risk, and a believable, specific failure scenario can be constructed or drawn from real cases.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The audience may find a third-party "surrogate" story less persuasive than being addressed directly (e.g., highly rational, cold, or skeptical B2B audiences), or where the story's specifics (names, exact figures) could appear fabricated or manipulative if not genuinely representative.
**Example From This Promotion:** "The trip was a disaster for RVers Bob and Helen Wallace... Two days, 800 miles, 90 gallons of gas later -- the Wallace's dreams were dashed. The park was closed."
