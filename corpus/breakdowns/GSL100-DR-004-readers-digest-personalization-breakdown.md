The literal subject is a sweepstakes entry attached to a record album offer. The marketing idea is that personalization converts an abstract, statistically remote prize into something that feels concrete and locally real — by naming the reader's own neighborhood, town, bank, and airport, the letter makes "you could win $250,000" feel less like a form-letter cliché and more like a plausible, specific event happening to *this* reader in *this* place. ## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle

**Observation:** The letter renders an abstract prize amount as multiple specific, sensory, socially-witnessed scenes using the recipient's real name, spouse's name, hometown, and street.
**Likely Reason:** Personalized, concrete imagery is processed and felt more vividly than an abstract number, and merged personal data increases the letter's felt legitimacy and relevance.
**General Rule:** When a variable-data mail system is available, use merged personal/geographic details to convert abstract benefit statements into vivid, individually-tailored scenes rather than generic hypotheticals.
**Use When:** Reliable personalization data (name, location, purchase history) is available and the offer benefits from felt specificity (prizes, milestones, local relevance).
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** Personalization data is thin, stale, or could misfire (wrong details), which would undermine rather than build credibility — evidence discipline requires the data actually be accurate and current.
**Example From This Promotion:** "think of the look on the faces of your West Chester neighbors if you drove down Johnny's way in a brand new car."

### Principle

**Observation:** The letter openly explains why the reader received the sweepstakes ("to call your attention to Reader's Digest products") rather than concealing the commercial motive.
**Likely Reason:** Transparent disclosure of intent preempts the reader's likely suspicion about why they're being mailed a sweepstakes, converting a potential objection into a credibility-building moment.
**General Rule:** When a promotional mechanic (sweepstakes, free gift, contest) is used as a vehicle for a commercial offer, state the rationale plainly rather than letting the reader infer a hidden agenda.
**Use When:** The commercial tie-in is genuinely reasonable and legally compliant (e.g., true no-purchase-necessary entry), so transparency costs nothing and builds trust.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The stated rationale would itself raise more objections than it resolves, or when regulatory/legal framing requires more careful language than a simple explanatory sentence.
**Example From This Promotion:** "Why were you sent this opportunity to enter our sweepstakes? To call your attention to Reader's Digest products...and in particular to the brand-new album we're offering."
