## 1. Big Idea

This letter's distinguishing move is using a physical, non-monetary object — a genuine but (to a U.S. reader) practically unspendable foreign banknote — as the literal opening subject of the letter, ahead of any financial claim. The first sentence names and evaluates the enclosure directly: "This authentic 100 Intis banknote from Peru is a great collector's item. But if you need real American money now to buy the things you deserve, you can get it quickly!" This single opening move accomplishes several things at once: it forces physical interaction with the mailing (the reader must have already picked up and looked at the enclosed note to make sense of this sentence), it creates an implicit contrast between something intriguing-but-useless and something the reader actually needs, and it sets up the entire letter's central reframe — the money the reader wants isn't a novelty item, it's real, and it's already sitting in their home's equity. The anthology's own commentary frames this directly: "This letter is a perfect illustration of how an inexpensive 'freemium' can lift readership and intensify the impact of what otherwise might have been a mundane, standard home equity loan mailing."

## 8. Mechanism Analysis

## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle 1

**Observation:** The letter encloses a genuine but functionally unspendable physical object (a foreign banknote) and makes that object the literal subject of its opening sentence, rather than opening with the financial offer directly.
**Likely Reason:** A physical object of ambiguous value forces the recipient to handle and evaluate the mailing before discarding it, and per the anthology's commentary, creates a mild sense of obligation to respond precisely because the object's worth "cannot immediately be determined."
**General Rule:** A low-cost, genuine physical premium of ambiguous or unknown value can meaningfully lift readership on an otherwise generic offer by forcing physical engagement and low-grade reciprocity/guilt before the sales argument begins.
**Use When:** The category is crowded with similar-looking offers (e.g., unsolicited loan mail) and a low-cost, novel physical item can be sourced and mailed economically at scale.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The physical premium could be perceived as deceptive about its actual value, or where the cost of sourcing and mailing a physical item at scale outweighs the readership lift — this pattern also requires the premium to be genuinely real (an actual banknote), not a simulated or printed imitation, to sustain the "authentic" framing.
**Example From This Promotion:** "This authentic 100 Intis banknote from Peru is a great collector's item. But if you need real American money now to buy the things you deserve, you can get it quickly!"

### Principle 2

**Observation:** An abstract available-credit figure is immediately followed by a varied list of concrete, disparate use cases rather than a single specified use.
**Likely Reason:** Different readers have different motivating wants; offering a spread of use cases (garage, pool, car, education, business) increases the odds that at least one resonates with any given reader's actual situation.
**General Rule:** When an offer is genuinely usable for many different purposes, list several concrete, varied use cases immediately after stating the available amount, rather than anchoring the pitch to a single assumed use.
**Use When:** The financial product or credit line is genuinely unrestricted in use and the target list is heterogeneous in likely motivations.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The product is actually restricted to a specific use (e.g., a purpose-built loan product) — listing unrelated use cases in that context would misrepresent the offer.
**Example From This Promotion:** "If you had $50,000 right now, what would you do with it? Build a garage, add a pool, buy a new car or boat, repay holiday expenses, pay for education, start a business?"
