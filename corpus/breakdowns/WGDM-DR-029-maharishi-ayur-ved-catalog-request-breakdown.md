## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle: Directly acknowledge and reframe an unfamiliar or potentially off-putting brand element rather than avoiding it

**Observation:** The letter dedicates an entire named subsection ("What's in a Name?") to openly acknowledging that its product and brand terminology sound unusual, then converts that acknowledgment into a claimed strength ("the names are just symbols of the true difference: the difference of power").
**Likely Reason:** An unaddressed, obviously-noticeable oddity (an unusual brand name, unfamiliar terminology) sits as an unspoken objection in the reader's mind throughout the letter regardless of whether the copy mentions it; naming the concern directly removes the reader's need to silently resolve it on their own (often unfavorably) and lets the writer control the reframe.
**General Rule:** When a product has an obvious, unavoidable point of friction that a skeptical reader will notice regardless (an unusual name, an unconventional origin, an unfamiliar category), address it directly and specifically rather than hoping the reader won't focus on it — and where possible, reframe the friction point itself as evidence of genuine differentiation.
**Use When:** The friction point is real, visible, and unavoidable, and the company genuinely has a substantive reason (heritage, authenticity, differentiated mechanism) that can support reframing it as a strength.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** The friction point is a genuine, unresolved weakness with no substantive counter-argument — reframing without substance risks reading as a dodge rather than a legitimate answer.
**Example From This Promotion:** The full "What's in a Name?" section, including the explicit contrast against typical hype phrases the company claims to avoid.

### Principle: Anchor a dramatic superiority claim to one specific, named, checkable source rather than multiple vague claims

**Observation:** The letter's entire potency argument rests on a single, precisely cited academic study (named institution, named journal, volume number, year) rather than a broader collection of looser claims.
**Likely Reason:** One highly specific, verifiable citation is more credible to a skeptical reader than several vague, unattributed claims, because specificity itself signals the writer has nothing to hide — a reader can, in principle, go look up the exact study.
**General Rule:** When making a dramatic comparative or superiority claim, prioritize anchoring it to a single, maximally specific, named, and dated source over presenting several vaguer supporting claims — depth and verifiability of one proof point often outweighs the breadth of several unverifiable ones.
**Use When:** A genuine, specific, citable source exists and actually supports the claim being made.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** No genuine study or source exists to cite, or the actual study's findings do not cleanly support the claim as stated — misrepresenting or overstating a real study's conclusions is a serious compliance and credibility risk.
**Example From This Promotion:** "Respected research — not done by us but conducted under rigorously controlled conditions by The Ohio State University College of Medicine and published in the respected journal, Pharmacology, Biochemistry and Behavior (Vol.43, 1992) — verifies this truth."
