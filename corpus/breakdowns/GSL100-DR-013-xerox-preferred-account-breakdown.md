## 1. Big Idea

The literal subject is an invitation to open a business credit account. The marketing idea is that the letter frames this not as Xerox asking the customer to apply for credit, but as Xerox recognizing something the customer already deserves ("We believe you should get credit for buying office products directly from Xerox") and pre-approving them for it. The entire persuasive burden of the letter rests on making a mundane B2B credit-account signup feel like a low-effort, already-decided privilege rather than an application requiring justification.

## 16. Reusable Principles

### Principle

**Observation:** The letter frames a standard credit-account signup as a privilege the reader has already been granted ("your instant line of credit has already been approved") rather than an application requiring qualification.
**Likely Reason:** Removing the psychological uncertainty of "will I qualify?" from an offer increases response by eliminating a common source of hesitation or self-disqualification in application-based offers.
**General Rule:** When an offer genuinely involves pre-qualification or pre-approval, state that fact explicitly and repeatedly, framing the recipient's action as acceptance of an already-decided privilege rather than submission of an application.
**Use When:** Pre-approval is genuine and can be stated truthfully without misleading the recipient about actual terms or conditions.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** Approval is not actually pre-determined, or terms are subject to change upon review, in which case this framing would be misleading and could damage trust or invite regulatory scrutiny.
**Example From This Promotion:** "Remember too, your instant line of credit has already been approved. So sign and mail your Acceptance Form today."

### Principle

**Observation:** The letter stacks three separate negations of common purchase objections into a single sentence: "No minimum purchase requirement. No fee or service charge. No risk or obligation whatsoever."
**Likely Reason:** Business buyers evaluating a new account or vendor relationship commonly anticipate hidden costs or commitments; addressing several likely objections back-to-back, in short declarative sentences, efficiently clears them without requiring the reader to ask.
**General Rule:** When an offer genuinely has no hidden costs or commitments, state each likely objection as a short, direct negation rather than one general reassurance sentence, so each specific fear is individually and explicitly addressed.
**Use When:** Each stated negation is factually accurate and the offer truly carries no such cost/commitment.
**Do Not Blindly Use When:** Any of the stated negations is only partially true (e.g., a fee applies under certain conditions) — such partial truths, if later discovered, undermine trust more than a single vaguer reassurance would.
**Example From This Promotion:** "There's no minimum purchase requirement. No fee or service charge. No risk or obligation whatsoever."

## 17. Worth Swiping

- Framing a standard signup/application offer as a pre-decided privilege the reader need only accept, when pre-approval is genuinely true.
- Stacking short, specific, individually stated negations of likely objections rather than one general reassurance line.
- Placing the single strongest concrete proof point (here, the specific credit-line dollar figure) after an itemized benefit list, so it lands as a culmination rather than an opening claim.
- Tying a soft deadline to concrete upcoming benefits (future discounts, a bonus product offer) rather than to artificial account scarcity.
- Repeating the core reassurance (no risk, already approved) immediately before the final call to action, at the exact point of hesitation.
